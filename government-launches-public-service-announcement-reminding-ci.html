Government Launches 'Public Service Announcement' Reminding Citizens To Love The President

Ad stamped 'Paid for by the U.S. Government' airs during NFL games, set to song called 'Love Me' — purely educational.

By Staff Reporter, Bureau of Government Affairs
INVALID DATE  ·  GOVMENT.ORG

Govment.org photo illustration  |  INVALID DATE

The White House confirmed this week that its expanding series of taxpayer-funded television advertisements — which feature the President, borrow visual imagery from his 2024 campaign, and are set to a song titled "Love Me" — are public service announcements, in the same tradition as the ones that told children not to do drugs and reminded motorists to buckle their seatbelts, except about loving the President.

The ads, which have aired during NFL broadcasts and carry the on-screen disclaimer "Paid for by the U.S. Government," are described by White House officials as purely educational in nature, intended to inform the American public about patriotism, national pride, and related concepts the administration declined to enumerate further. The Bureau of Strategic Presidential Awareness did not respond to a request for clarification, as it does not exist, though the underlying situation does.

"This is no different from any other public service announcement," a senior administration official explained, in the sense that it is paid for by the public, and it is an announcement, and it is, at minimum, a service to someone. Officials noted that the word "campaign" can mean many things, including a public health campaign, an awareness campaign, or a campaign to become President of the United States, and that conflating these definitions would be both irresponsible and unfair.

The song selected to underscore the footage, "Love Me," was chosen following what the Office of Patriotic Media Procurement described as a "competitive process," the details of which remain classified at a level typically reserved for submarine coordinates. Music licensing experts confirmed that the title of a song used in a government advertisement has no bearing on the content, intent, or legal status of that advertisement, a position they maintained for approximately four seconds before declining further comment.

A Republican senator, when asked about the ads, told reporters there "probably is a rule" against using federal funds to produce content that promotes the sitting president personally, a statement the Bureau of Congressional Epistemic Confidence rated as among the most carefully hedged assessments delivered on the floor of the United States Senate in the modern era. The senator did not specify what the rule was, who enforced it, or whether anyone would be doing so in the foreseeable future.

Historians of government communication noted that the PSA format has a distinguished lineage, including Smokey Bear, who was created in 1944 and has his own federal statute — 18 U.S.C. § 711 — making it a criminal offense to use his image without authorization from the Forest Service. Smokey Bear has not, to date, aired during Monday Night Football set to a song about loving Smokey Bear, though officials stressed this comparison was not instructive.

Citizens who viewed the advertisements during the NFL games in question were not surveyed on whether they found them informative, patriotic, or educational, as no such mechanism exists. A spokesperson for the Administration noted that the American people's reaction to the ads could be observed indirectly through the democratic process, the next scheduled occurrence of which is approximately three and a half years away.

The White House announced that additional public service announcements are currently in production, covering such urgent civic topics as the President's achievements, the President's vision, and, according to one internal document obtained and immediately disputed by three separate agencies, the President. These too will be stamped "Paid for by the U.S. Government," which officials confirmed is accurate.
